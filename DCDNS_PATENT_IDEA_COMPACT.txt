================================================================================
0. RELATIONSHIP TO THE VALIDATED SOLUTION
================================================================================

The repository now distinguishes two responses to the acknowledged-write
stale-majority problem:

  B - Publication barrier (write path, primary fix)
      A write is acknowledged only after a majority of registry nodes reports
      applying the committed revision to the state used for DNS answers. B is
      the standard commit/apply acknowledgement discipline. It is implemented
      behind --publication-barrier; the audit recommends making it the default,
      but the code keeps it opt-in.

  C - RAM-DNS (read path, optional detector)
      Each DNS answer carries revision evidence; the resolver requires a
      semantic majority plus a freshness condition against a watermark. C
      detects a stale majority and fails closed (or marks the answer stale). It
      does not repair the write path.

This disclosure is about C, not B. B is not claimed as an invention. The audit
and the academic review conclude that:

  - the stale-DNS phenomenon is real and operationally significant;
  - the local DCDNS bug is real and deterministically reproducible;
  - B is the high-leverage fix;
  - C is a plausible defence-in-depth detector;
  - C's novelty and incremental benefit over B are not demonstrated;
  - a professional prior-art search is required before any filing.

The patent-style claims below should therefore be read as candidate claim
language for the DNS-specific encoding and policy combination, not as a
statement that the combination is patentable.


================================================================================
1. TITLE
================================================================================

Freshness-Coherent Quorum Resolution for DNS Answers from Replicated
Authoritative Nodes

Short name: Revision-Aware Majority DNS (RAM-DNS)


================================================================================
2. ONE-PARAGRAPH SUMMARY
================================================================================

A replicated registry runs an authoritative DNS responder on every node. Each
node answers from the registry revision it has committed. A client-facing
resolver asks all nodes and returns an answer only when a majority agrees. The
candidate mechanism attaches the node's serving revision to each DNS answer and
requires two conditions before returning it: (a) semantic majority agreement on
the DNS answer, and (b) a freshness condition relative to the highest committed
revision observed. If a majority agrees on an old answer, the resolver retries
briefly and then returns SERVFAIL, or a deliberately marked stale answer, instead
of silently serving stale data. This is a read-side detector. It complements,
but does not replace, a write-path publication barrier.


================================================================================
3. PROBLEM
================================================================================

3.1 The failure that majority voting misses
-------------------------------------------
The semantic-only resolver normalizes each DNS response to (RCODE, answer
records), groups identical answers, and returns a group only if it has at least
2 of 3 votes. That resists one lying or unavailable node.

It does not resist a stale majority:

  Revision 10: techfest.cs.test -> 10.20.5.42
  Revision 11: techfest.cs.test -> 10.20.5.99

  n1: revision 10 -> 10.20.5.42   (lagging)
  n2: revision 10 -> 10.20.5.42   (lagging)
  n3: revision 11 -> 10.20.5.99   (current)

  Semantic-only resolver: 2 votes for 10.20.5.42, 1 for 10.20.5.99
  Result:                  returns 10.20.5.42  (STALE)

The registry has already committed the new address, but the client receives the
old one because the majority is behind. This is a correctness gap, not a
performance issue. It matters most just after an update, during replication lag,
after a node restart, during a partial network partition, or when a responder is
frozen at an older snapshot.

3.2 The local audit
-------------------
The DCDNS consistency audit found a concrete cause in the baseline write path.
The coordinator acknowledged a write when a majority had STORED the entry, while
followers APPLIED it only on a later replication push. In the final baseline run,
12 of 12 acknowledged writes left a stale semantic majority; a natural run
reproduced the failure in 20 of 20 trials. The audit's primary fix is B, the
publication barrier.

C addresses a related but distinct question: once a client queries DNS, can the
resolver tell whether the agreeing majority is answering from a sufficiently
current committed revision? C does not fix the write path, and on the unfixed
baseline it converted every stale window into SERVFAIL (12 of 12) rather than
returning a stale answer.

3.3 Why existing mechanisms do not solve it
-------------------------------------------
- DNSSEC proves that an answer was signed and came from a zone key. It does not
  compare replicas or prove that the answer reflects the latest committed
  registry revision.
- Primary/secondary DNS and anycast provide redundancy and availability. They do
  not let a resolver compare independent committed states and vote.
- Distributed databases use quorum reads with version numbers, but they do not
  operate on DNS wire responses or group DNS answers semantically.
- Blockchain DNS systems (Namecoin, Handshake, ENS) reach consensus on name
  ownership. They do not solve per-query freshness across independently
  committed authoritative DNS nodes.
- The DCDNS prototype implements semantic majority voting and revision-coherent
  freshness. The freshness combination is the candidate delta, but its novelty
  is unproven.

3.4 Known prior art
-------------------
The underlying pattern is well known. PQR (HotStorage 2019) states that "a bare
quorum read is insufficient" and needs a rinse phase for pending updates. Raft
(USENIX ATC 2014) separates commit from apply and warns that read-only requests
can return stale data without additional precautions. RFC 1034 accepts DNS
propagation delay; RFC 1996 exists because zone transfers can leave "long
intervals of incoherence among authority servers." Gao and Venkataramani
(INFOCOM 2019) measured stale DNS behavior across eight managed providers, and
Akamai DNS (SIGCOMM 2020) documents stale metadata as a production failure mode.
The academic review in docs/ACADEMIC_REVIEW.md records the sources and the
verification level. This disclosure does not claim that the problem or the
general fix is new.


================================================================================
4. THE INVENTION
================================================================================

4.1 Core idea
-------------
Attach a committed revision identifier to every DNS answer and make the resolver
require both:

  (a) semantic majority agreement on the DNS answer; and
  (b) a freshness condition relative to a freshness watermark.

If the majority answer is stale, the resolver must not silently return it. It
may retry for a bounded time, then return SERVFAIL, or return an explicitly
marked stale answer under a policy that prefers availability.

4.2 System components
---------------------
A. Replicated registry
   Multiple registry nodes commit an ordered log of signed operations. Each
   committed operation advances a monotonically increasing revision number.

B. Per-node authoritative DNS responder
   Each registry node serves a zone snapshot derived from its own committed
   state. The snapshot is bound to the node's current serving revision.

C. Revision metadata channel
   The revision may be carried by one of:
     - a custom EDNS(0) option in the DNS response;
     - a companion status query to the node's HTTP endpoint;
     - a signed revision token binding the answer hash, revision and node ID.
   The EDNS(0) option keeps answer and revision in one packet. The status query
   is easier to prototype. The signed token is stronger but needs key management.

D. Quorum resolver
   The resolver sends the query to all configured nodes, collects answers and
   revision metadata, normalises the answers, groups them, and applies both the
   quorum and freshness tests.

E. Freshness watermark
   A revision value representing the best known committed state. Examples:
     - maximum revision among reachable nodes;
     - revision confirmed by a quorum of registry status endpoints;
     - revision supplied by a trusted registry coordinator;
     - client-supplied minimum revision for read-your-writes semantics.

F. Policy engine
   Configuration for quorum size, maximum allowed lag (delta), retry budget,
   behaviour on a stale majority, and whether negative answers are subject to
   the same freshness rule.

4.3 Method steps
----------------
1. Each node i maintains committed revision R_i and a DNS snapshot Z_i derived
   from that revision.

2. A client sends a DNS query Q for name N and type T to the resolver.

3. The resolver sends Q to each node in a configured set S.

4. Each node returns a DNS response A_i and the serving revision R_i used to
   produce it.

5. The resolver normalises each A_i into a canonical semantic form:
   RCODE, answer record type/class/RDATA, records sorted canonically. The
   transaction ID, TTL and harmless flags are excluded or normalised.

6. The resolver groups nodes by identical canonical answer.

7. The resolver identifies a candidate majority group M whose size is at least
   the quorum threshold (for N nodes, floor(N/2) + 1; 2 of 3 in the prototype).

8. The resolver computes a freshness watermark W from the revision identifiers.

9. The resolver evaluates a freshness condition F:
   F1: at least one node in M has R_i >= W;
   F2: at least a quorum of M has R_i >= W - delta;
   F3: the minimum revision in M is >= W - delta.

10. If M exists and F is true, return the majority answer to the client,
    restoring the client's transaction ID and applying the resolver's TTL
    policy.

11. If M exists but F is false, retry for a bounded time; if the nodes converge,
    return the fresh answer; otherwise return SERVFAIL or a marked stale answer
    according to policy.

12. If no majority exists, return SERVFAIL as in the current design.

13. Record metrics: watermark, per-node revision, majority group, stale-majority
    count, retries and final decision.

4.4 Why the freshness test is not trivial
-----------------------------------------
A naive resolver could simply return the highest-revision answer. That is wrong:

- The highest-revision node might be the liar. Revision says how much log the
  node applied, not whether its answer is correct.
- A single node could claim a fake high revision. The revision must be bound to
  the node's committed state, ideally by a signature or a quorum-confirmed
  watermark.
- Requiring every node to be at the same revision destroys availability during
  normal replication lag.

The mechanism therefore combines majority agreement on content with a freshness
condition. It can tolerate a bounded lag delta instead of demanding perfect
synchronisation. It still cannot make a false revision claim true; signed tokens
prove authorship, not correctness.

4.5 Alternative embodiments
---------------------------
- Revision carried in a custom EDNS(0) option.
- Revision carried in a companion TXT record or reserved meta-query.
- Revision obtained from a separate status API.
- Revision signed by the node and verified by the resolver.
- Freshness applied only to positive answers, or to both positive and negative
  answers.
- Stale answer returned with a short TTL and a warning flag when availability
  is more important than freshness.
- Client-supplied minimum revision for read-your-writes after an update.
- Per-node lag ledger; persistently lagging nodes are removed from the quorum
  calculation.

5. WORKED EXAMPLE
================================================================================

Initial state, revision 10:
  n1: 10.20.5.42, rev 10
  n2: 10.20.5.42, rev 10
  n3: 10.20.5.42, rev 10

Update commits on n3 first, revision 11:
  n1: 10.20.5.42, rev 10   (lagging)
  n2: 10.20.5.42, rev 10   (lagging)
  n3: 10.20.5.99, rev 11   (current)

Semantic-only resolver:
  majority = 10.20.5.42 (2 votes)
  returns 10.20.5.42       <- stale

RAM-DNS resolver:
  majority = 10.20.5.42 (2 votes)
  watermark = 11
  freshness condition fails because the majority group has no revision 11
  retry for a bounded time
  if n1/n2 catch up -> return 10.20.5.99
  if they do not converge -> SERVFAIL (or marked stale answer)

This gives the client a clear choice: a fresh answer, an explicit failure, or an
explicitly marked stale answer. It never silently presents old data as current.

With the publication barrier B, the earlier write acknowledgement would have
waited until a majority applied revision 11. The normal-path stale window would
not exist. C remains relevant for a responder that is frozen after B acknowledged,
or for a deployment that cannot enforce B.

7. PRIOR ART AND DIFFERENTIATION
================================================================================

This is a starting map, not a complete prior-art search.

DNS redundancy
  Primary/secondary authoritative servers and anycast provide availability. A
  resolver normally trusts one authoritative response. They do not compare
  multiple answers and do not apply a majority vote.

DNSSEC
  Proves authenticity and integrity of a signed zone. It does not compare
  replicas or prove that the answer reflects the latest committed registry
  revision.

Distributed database quorum reads
  Use version numbers and quorum reads, but do not operate on DNS wire
  responses or group DNS answers semantically. PQR (2019) and Raft (2014)
  establish that a bare quorum read can be stale and that extra steps are
  needed. Those are the closest conceptual references and they cut against a
  broad novelty claim.

DNS consistency research
  DNScup targets cache consistency with leases; ECO-DNS optimizes TTLs against
  an expected-inconsistency metric; Aeacus uses QUIC handshakes for 5G name
  resolution. They are adjacent, not identical, and they show that DNS
  consistency is an active area.

Measurement and operations
  Gao and Venkataramani (INFOCOM 2019) measured update inconsistency across
  managed DNS providers. Akamai DNS (SIGCOMM 2020) documents stale metadata and
  partial connectivity as production failure modes. These establish that the
  problem is real, not that RAM-DNS is new.

Blockchain DNS
  Namecoin, Handshake and ENS consensus on ownership. They do not provide
  per-query freshness across independently committed authoritative DNS nodes.

Current DCDNS prototype
  Implements semantic majority voting and revision-coherent freshness: signed
  EDNS(0) revision metadata, F1/F2/F3 conditions, bounded retry and fail-closed
  stale-majority handling. The freshness combination is the candidate delta.

The novelty argument is therefore narrow and unproven: the combination of
(i) semantic DNS answer grouping, (ii) revision metadata bound to each answer,
and (iii) a freshness condition that can reject a stale majority and fail
closed. Each ingredient is known. The academic review found no exact prior art
for the name or the full combination, but also found no evidence of novelty.
Absence of an exact term is not proof of novelty.

8. VALIDATION AND EVIDENCE
================================================================================

The existing DCDNS testbed has been used to test the idea without inventing a
new system.

Experiment: stale majority (implemented in dcdns/experiments.py)
  1. Start three registry/DNS nodes at the same revision.
  2. Freeze the real DNS snapshots of n1 and n2 at their committed revision.
  3. Commit a signed address change; n3 applies it, n1/n2 keep serving the
     frozen snapshot.
  4. Query the semantic-only resolver: it returns the stale address.
  5. Query the RAM-DNS resolver: it detects that the majority group has no node
     at the watermark revision.
  6. Confirm that it retries and then returns SERVFAIL, or a marked stale
     answer if policy allows.
  7. Thaw n1/n2, let them catch up, and confirm the fresh answer is returned.

Measured in the live run:
  - semantic-only baseline returned the stale address;
  - RAM-DNS reported stale-majority at the next revision and returned SERVFAIL;
  - the new answer was served after thaw;
  - all 12 live experiments passed, including the stale-majority case.

Controlled A/B/C comparison (docs/CONSISTENCY_AUDIT.md):
  - Baseline A: 12/12 acknowledged writes left a stale semantic majority;
    natural run 20/20.
  - Barrier B: 3/3 control writes acknowledged with 0 stale; 9/9 held writes
    timed out NO_QUORUM with ambiguous: true; all 9 converged after release.
  - Detector C: 0/12 stale answers, 12/12 SERVFAIL, 24 retries, lookup p50
    117.84 ms.

Test evidence:
  - 108/108 tests pass, including 10 commit-hold/barrier tests and 37 RAM-DNS
    tests;
  - 9/9 cluster checks, 14/14 portal flow checks, 12/12 live experiments.

Implementation in the existing code:
  - EDNS(0) option 65001 in dcdns/dns_server.py, with optional Ed25519
    signature and zone digest.
  - Freshness check F1/F2/F3 in dcdns/quorum_resolver.py.
  - Stale-majority experiment in dcdns/experiments.py.
  - Unit tests in tests/test_ram_dns.py for F1/F2/F3, no-majority, retries,
    marked-stale policy, token verification and real snapshot freeze.
  - Barrier tests in tests/test_commit_hold.py.

The evidence demonstrates that C works as a detector in this testbed. It does
not demonstrate that C is novel, that it improves outcomes over B, or that it
generalizes to a production DNS deployment.

9. LIMITATIONS AND HONEST ASSESSMENT
================================================================================

- B, not C, is the primary fix for the acknowledged-write stale-majority
  problem. B is a standard commit/apply publication barrier and is not claimed
  as an invention.
- The idea is incremental over quorum reads, DNSSEC, primary/secondary DNS,
  anycast, DNS consistency research and blockchain DNS. Prior art is crowded.
- The prototype implements revision metadata and the freshness condition, but
  the evidence is a three-node lab deployment on one host, not a production
  measurement.
- Revision metadata must itself be trustworthy. A malicious node could claim a
  fake revision unless the revision is signed or confirmed by a quorum. A
  signature proves authorship, not truth.
- Two colluding DNS nodes can still defeat majority trust. This is not Byzantine
  fault tolerance.
- A bounded lag policy trades freshness against availability. The correct delta
  is deployment-specific.
- C's measured local cost is high: 12/12 SERVFAIL and a lookup p50 of about
  118 ms on the baseline cluster.
- C's watermark is incomplete: if every responder is still at revision R, C
  cannot know that R+1 exists.
- B does not cover a frozen responder because it checks registry dns_revision,
  not the responder's serving_revision.
- The idea has more educational and research value than demonstrated commercial
  value. It is a reasonable college disclosure, not a likely standalone product
  patent.
- Do not publish the specific mechanism before checking the college's IP and
  public-disclosure rules.
